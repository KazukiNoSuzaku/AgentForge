# AI Regulatory Frameworks: A Comparative Analysis of the EU, US, and China

## Executive Summary

Three major global powers have adopted fundamentally distinct approaches to governing artificial intelligence. The European Union has enacted the world's first comprehensive, binding AI legislation through the AI Act, which employs a risk-based classification system [1]. The United States relies on a fragmented combination of voluntary frameworks, executive orders, and sector-specific guidance rather than omnibus legislation [2][3]. China has pursued sector-specific regulations targeting recommendation algorithms, deepfakes, and generative AI, with an emphasis on content control and national security [4][5]. These diverging philosophies reflect deeper political economies: the EU prioritizes harmonized consumer protection, the US favors innovation with light-touch oversight, and China emphasizes state control alongside technological advancement.

---

## Introduction

Artificial intelligence has emerged as a defining technology of the twenty-first century, raising profound questions about accountability, safety, and democratic governance. Governments worldwide face a common dilemma: how to foster innovation while mitigating risks ranging from algorithmic bias to autonomous weapons systems. The regulatory responses of the European Union, United States, and China offer a natural comparative experiment — three major technological powers with different political systems, legal traditions, and economic priorities have arrived at starkly different approaches.

This report examines the key provisions of each jurisdiction's regulatory framework, identifies cross-cutting patterns and contradictions, and assesses the potential for convergence or fragmentation in the emerging global AI governance landscape.

---

## The EU AI Act: A Risk-Based, Binding Framework

The EU Artificial Intelligence Act, which entered into force in August 2024, represents the world's first comprehensive binding regulation of AI systems [1]. Its central innovation is a **risk-based classification** scheme that stratifies AI applications into four tiers:

- **Unacceptable risk**: Banned outright (e.g., real-time biometric surveillance in public spaces, social scoring by governments)
- **High risk**: Subject to mandatory conformity assessments, transparency requirements, and registration in an EU database (e.g., AI in hiring, credit scoring, medical devices, critical infrastructure)
- **Limited risk**: Subject to transparency obligations (e.g., chatbots must identify as AI)
- **Minimal risk**: No specific obligations

**General-purpose AI (GPAI) models** — including large language models — are subject to a separate regime requiring technical documentation, copyright compliance, and, for the most capable "systemic risk" models, adversarial testing and incident reporting [1].

The Act also establishes AI regulatory sandboxes to foster innovation, and prohibits post-market manipulation of AI systems in ways that circumvent the regulation. Penalties reach €35 million or 7% of global annual turnover for the most serious violations [1].

*Key assessment:* The EU AI Act's strength lies in its horizontal scope and binding nature. However, critics argue that the compliance burden is disproportionate for smaller developers, and the risk classification process lacks granularity for rapidly evolving capabilities [6].

## United States: Fragmented but Evolving Governance

Unlike the EU, the United States has not enacted omnibus federal AI legislation as of early 2025. Governance instead relies on a patchwork of instruments [2]:

**Executive Action**: President Biden's Executive Order 14110 (October 2023) directed federal agencies to develop sector-specific AI risk guidance, required developers of the most powerful models to share safety test results with the government, and established the AI Safety Institute within NIST [3].

**Voluntary Frameworks**: The NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0, January 2023) provides voluntary guidance for organizations to identify, assess, and manage AI risks. Adoption is broad but uneven; there is no enforcement mechanism [2].

**Sector-Specific Regulation**: The FDA, FTC, EEOC, and financial regulators have each issued guidance or enforcement actions applying existing laws to AI in their domains. This creates jurisdiction-specific rules but no unified standard [2].

**State-Level Activity**: California, Colorado, Texas, and other states have enacted or are pursuing AI-specific legislation, creating potential compliance complexity for interstate operators [3].

The Biden EO was substantially modified by the Trump administration in January 2025, which revoked several provisions related to equity and safety testing mandates, signaling a shift toward deregulation. Congressional AI legislation remains stalled despite bipartisan interest [3].

*Key assessment:* The US approach maximizes flexibility for innovators but creates regulatory uncertainty. The absence of federal preemption means businesses must navigate a patchwork of state laws and agency guidance — a significant compliance challenge for multinational operators.

## China: Sector-Specific Regulation with a Security Emphasis

China has pursued a distinctive regulatory strategy: enacting targeted rules for specific AI modalities rather than a single overarching law, while embedding AI governance within its broader national security and content regulation frameworks [4].

Key regulations include:

- **Algorithm Recommendation Regulations** (effective March 2022): Requires providers of recommendation systems to register with the government, allow users to opt out of personalization, and avoid using algorithms to induce addiction or manipulate prices [4]
- **Deep Synthesis Regulations** (effective January 2023): Mandates labeling of AI-generated content (deepfakes) and requires users to verify their real identities [5]
- **Interim Measures for Generative AI Services** (effective August 2023): Requires security assessments for generative AI services before launch, mandates that training data comply with Chinese law (including content restrictions), and holds providers liable for AI-generated content [5]

China's approach is notable for its emphasis on **content compliance** — AI systems must not generate content that undermines state authority — and its **pre-launch security review** requirements, which have no equivalent in the EU or US [4][5].

*Key assessment:* China's regulatory cadence is fast but creates compliance burdens for foreign entrants. The content restrictions have been criticized by civil society groups as tools for censorship alongside legitimate safety goals [6].

## Cross-Cutting Patterns and Contradictions

### Convergence: Risk-Based Thinking

All three jurisdictions employ some version of risk-tiering, even if implemented differently. The EU AI Act's four-tier classification, the US NIST AI RMF's risk management approach, and China's emphasis on "high-risk" AI requiring security assessments reflect a shared analytical framework — despite different enforcement mechanisms [1][2][4].

### Divergence: Mandatory vs. Voluntary

The most fundamental divide is between the EU's binding mandatory requirements and the US's preference for voluntary frameworks. This creates an asymmetric regulatory burden: EU-registered companies face strict pre-deployment requirements; US companies benefit from greater flexibility but face reputational risk from self-certification [1][2].

### Contradiction: Innovation vs. Precaution

Stakeholders consistently report tension between innovation-enabling and precautionary regulatory goals [6]. The EU's conformity assessment requirements have been criticized for potentially excluding smaller innovators from high-risk AI markets. The US approach is criticized for creating a "race to the bottom" dynamic where market pressure discourages voluntary safety investments.

---

## Implications for International AI Governance

The divergence across these three major AI powers raises important questions about regulatory fragmentation:

**The Brussels Effect**: The EU AI Act may function as a de facto global standard, as multinationals find it more efficient to comply with the strictest jurisdiction globally. Evidence from GDPR implementation suggests this dynamic is plausible [1][6].

**Mutual Recognition vs. Fragmentation**: Currently, there is no binding international AI governance framework. The G7 Hiroshima AI Process and the OECD AI Principles provide soft-law guidance, but enforcement remains national [3].

**China's Regulatory Influence**: China's regulations may influence the Global South, where Chinese technology companies operate and Chinese regulatory templates may be adopted as part of technology partnerships [4].

---

## Conclusion

The EU, US, and China represent three distinct regulatory philosophies for governing AI: mandatory horizontal regulation (EU), voluntary sector-specific guidance (US), and targeted state-security-oriented rules (China). Each approach reflects legitimate policy priorities and faces real limitations.

The most significant near-term governance challenge is the absence of international coordination mechanisms. As AI systems become more capable and globally deployed, regulatory divergence creates compliance costs for developers and potential arbitrage opportunities that undermine the goals of all three frameworks. Whether the emerging global regime converges toward a Brussels-Effect-driven EU standard, a multi-polar patchwork, or a novel international framework remains one of the defining policy questions of the decade.
