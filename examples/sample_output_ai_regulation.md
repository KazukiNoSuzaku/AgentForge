## Executive Summary

Three major global powers have adopted fundamentally distinct approaches to governing artificial intelligence. The European Union has enacted the world's first comprehensive, binding AI legislation through the AI Act, employing a risk-based classification system that categorizes applications into four tiers [1]. The United States relies on a fragmented patchwork of voluntary frameworks, executive orders, and sector-specific guidance rather than omnibus federal legislation [2][3]. China has pursued sector-specific regulations targeting recommendation algorithms, deepfakes, and generative AI, with a pronounced emphasis on content control and national security [4][5]. These diverging strategies reflect deeper governance philosophies: the EU prioritizes harmonized consumer protection, the US favors innovation-friendly flexibility, and China emphasizes state oversight alongside rapid technological deployment.

## Introduction

Artificial intelligence has emerged as a defining technology of the twenty-first century, raising profound questions about accountability, safety, and democratic governance. Governments worldwide face a common dilemma: how to foster innovation while mitigating risks ranging from algorithmic bias to autonomous weapons systems. The regulatory responses of the European Union, United States, and China offer a natural comparative experiment — three major technological powers with different political systems, legal traditions, and economic priorities have arrived at starkly different frameworks.

This report examines the key provisions of each jurisdiction's approach, identifies cross-cutting patterns and contradictions, and assesses the potential for convergence or fragmentation in the emerging global AI governance landscape.

## The EU AI Act: A Risk-Based, Binding Framework

The EU Artificial Intelligence Act, which entered into force in August 2024, represents the world's first comprehensive binding regulation of AI systems [1]. Its central innovation is a risk-based classification scheme that stratifies AI applications into four tiers:

- **Unacceptable risk**: Banned outright — including real-time biometric surveillance in public spaces, social scoring by governments, and manipulative AI targeting vulnerable populations
- **High risk**: Subject to mandatory conformity assessments, transparency requirements, human oversight obligations, and registration in an EU database — covering AI in hiring, credit scoring, medical devices, law enforcement, and critical infrastructure
- **Limited risk**: Transparency obligations only — chatbots and AI-generated content must be labeled as such
- **Minimal risk**: No specific regulatory obligations

General-purpose AI models, including large language models, are subject to a separate regime requiring technical documentation, copyright compliance, and — for the most capable "systemic risk" models — adversarial testing, incident reporting, and cybersecurity evaluations [1]. Penalties for non-compliance reach EUR 35 million or 7% of global annual turnover, whichever is higher.

The Act also establishes AI regulatory sandboxes to support controlled innovation, and mandates that the European AI Office coordinate enforcement across member states [6]. However, critics argue the compliance burden disproportionately affects smaller developers, and the static risk classification may struggle to keep pace with rapid capability advances [8].

## United States: Voluntary Frameworks and Sectoral Guidance

The United States has not enacted omnibus federal AI legislation as of early 2025. Governance instead relies on a layered combination of executive action, voluntary standards, and existing regulatory authority [2]:

**Executive Action.** President Biden's Executive Order 14110 (October 2023) directed federal agencies to develop sector-specific AI risk guidance, required developers of the most powerful models to share safety test results with the government, and established the AI Safety Institute within NIST [3]. The Trump administration substantially revised this order in January 2025, revoking several provisions related to equity requirements and mandatory safety testing.

**Voluntary Frameworks.** The NIST AI Risk Management Framework (AI RMF 1.0, January 2023) provides voluntary guidance for organizations to identify, assess, and manage AI risks across the lifecycle. Adoption has been widespread but uneven, with no enforcement mechanism beyond market incentive [2].

**Sector-Specific Enforcement.** The FDA, FTC, EEOC, and financial regulators have each issued guidance or brought enforcement actions applying existing statutory authority to AI in their domains. This creates jurisdiction-specific rules but no unified national standard [2][3].

**State-Level Activity.** California, Colorado, Texas, and Illinois have enacted or are pursuing AI-specific legislation covering areas from automated employment decisions to facial recognition. This state-level activity creates potential compliance complexity for operators serving multiple jurisdictions [3].

The fundamental tension in the US approach is between maximizing innovation flexibility and providing regulatory certainty. Businesses must navigate a patchwork of agency interpretations and state requirements with no federal preemption mechanism.

## China: Sector-Specific Regulation with a Security Emphasis

China has pursued a distinctive regulatory strategy: enacting targeted rules for specific AI modalities rather than a single overarching framework, while embedding AI governance within its broader content regulation and national security infrastructure [4].

Three landmark regulations define the current landscape:

**Algorithm Recommendation Regulations** (effective March 2022): Require providers of recommendation systems to register with the Cyberspace Administration of China, allow users to opt out of algorithmic personalization, and prohibit using algorithms to manipulate prices, induce addiction, or discriminate against consumers [4].

**Deep Synthesis Regulations** (effective January 2023): Mandate labeling of all AI-generated content including deepfakes, require providers to verify user identities, and establish content moderation obligations [5].

**Interim Measures for Generative AI Services** (effective August 2023): Require security assessments before generative AI services can be offered to the public, mandate that training data comply with Chinese law including content restrictions, and hold providers legally liable for outputs [5][7].

China's approach is notable for its speed of implementation and its emphasis on pre-launch review — generative AI services must pass government security assessments before becoming available, a requirement with no equivalent in the EU or US frameworks [4][5]. The content compliance requirements, which prohibit AI outputs that "undermine state authority," have drawn criticism from international civil society organizations as tools for censorship alongside legitimate safety objectives [8].

## Cross-Cutting Analysis

### Convergence: Risk-Based Thinking

Despite different implementation mechanisms, all three jurisdictions employ risk-tiering as an organizing principle. The EU AI Act's four-tier classification, the NIST AI RMF's contextual risk management approach, and China's targeted focus on "high-impact" AI modalities reflect a shared analytical framework [1][2][4]. This convergence around risk-based governance may provide a foundation for future international coordination.

### Divergence: Mandatory vs. Voluntary Compliance

The most consequential divide is between binding requirements and voluntary guidance. EU-registered companies face mandatory pre-deployment conformity assessments for high-risk AI; US companies operate under voluntary frameworks supplemented by agency-specific enforcement; Chinese companies must obtain government approval before launch. Each model creates different incentive structures for safety investment [1][2][5].

### The Cost of Fragmentation

Multinational AI developers face a "regulatory trilemma": complying simultaneously with EU binding requirements, US voluntary-but-enforced guidance, and Chinese content and security mandates creates significant operational overhead. Preliminary estimates suggest compliance costs for a frontier AI company operating across all three jurisdictions could reach $10–50 million annually [8].

## Implications and Outlook

**The Brussels Effect.** The EU AI Act may function as a de facto global standard, as multinationals find it more efficient to meet the strictest jurisdiction's requirements globally. Evidence from GDPR's global influence supports this hypothesis, though the AI Act's complexity may limit its extraterritorial reach compared to data protection regulation [1][8].

**International Coordination.** The G7 Hiroshima AI Process, OECD AI Principles, and the UK AI Safety Summit (2023) represent initial soft-law coordination efforts, but no binding international framework is imminent. The US-EU Trade and Technology Council has discussed regulatory alignment without achieving convergence [3].

**Regulatory Competition.** China's fast regulatory cadence may influence the Global South, where Chinese technology companies operate extensively and where Chinese regulatory templates may be adopted as part of technology partnership agreements [4].

## Conclusion

The EU, US, and China represent three archetypal approaches to AI governance: mandatory horizontal regulation, voluntary sector-specific guidance, and state-security-oriented targeted rules. Each reflects legitimate policy priorities and confronts real limitations. The most pressing near-term challenge is the absence of robust international coordination — as AI systems become more capable and globally deployed, regulatory divergence imposes compliance costs on developers and creates arbitrage opportunities that may undermine all three frameworks. Whether the global AI governance regime converges toward an EU-led standard, a multipolar patchwork, or a novel international framework remains one of the defining policy questions of the decade.
