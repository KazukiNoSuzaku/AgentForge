## The Quantum Threat to Public-Key Cryptography

### Shor's Algorithm

Peter Shor's 1994 algorithm demonstrated that a sufficiently large quantum computer can factor integers and compute discrete logarithms in polynomial time [1]. This directly undermines three foundational assumptions of modern cryptography:

**RSA** relies on the computational hardness of factoring the product of two large primes. A quantum computer running Shor's algorithm could factor a 2048-bit RSA modulus in hours, compared to the billions of years required classically [1][2].

**Elliptic Curve Cryptography (ECC)**, including ECDSA and ECDH, relies on the discrete logarithm problem over elliptic curve groups. Shor's algorithm solves this with comparable efficiency, rendering ECC-256 and ECC-384 insecure against quantum adversaries [2].

**Diffie-Hellman Key Exchange**, the foundation of TLS session negotiation, is similarly vulnerable. A quantum adversary capable of breaking Diffie-Hellman could retroactively decrypt any recorded TLS session that did not use ephemeral keys [1][3].

### Grover's Algorithm and Symmetric Cryptography

Grover's 1996 search algorithm provides a quadratic speedup for brute-force key search, effectively halving the security level of symmetric ciphers [2]. AES-128, currently considered secure with 128-bit strength, would offer only 64-bit security against a quantum adversary — below acceptable thresholds. AES-256, however, retains 128-bit quantum security and remains recommended for long-term confidentiality [2][4]. Hash functions are similarly affected: SHA-256 offers 128-bit collision resistance against quantum attacks, which remains adequate for most applications.

## Timeline Estimates: When Is the Threat Real?

The critical question for practitioners is when a cryptographically relevant quantum computer will exist. Current estimates vary significantly [3][6]:

**Optimistic (2033–2037).** IBM, Google, and several government agencies project that error-corrected quantum computers capable of running Shor's algorithm on cryptographically relevant key sizes could emerge within a decade. IBM's roadmap targets 100,000+ qubits by 2033 [6].

**Consensus (2035–2040).** The Global Risk Institute's annual survey of quantum computing experts places the median estimate for a 50% probability of breaking RSA-2048 at approximately 2037 [3].

**Conservative (2040–2050+).** Skeptics note that current quantum computers operate with hundreds to low thousands of noisy qubits, while breaking RSA-2048 requires an estimated 4,000+ logical (error-corrected) qubits — each composed of thousands of physical qubits. The engineering gap remains substantial [6].

Regardless of the timeline, the "harvest now, decrypt later" (HNDL) threat demands immediate action. Nation-state adversaries are widely believed to be recording encrypted communications today with the intent of decrypting them once quantum capability matures [3][5]. Data with confidentiality requirements extending beyond 2035 — including government secrets, medical records, and financial data — is already at risk under this model.

## Post-Quantum Cryptography Standards

### The NIST Standardization Process

In August 2024, NIST published the first three post-quantum cryptographic standards after an eight-year public evaluation process involving 82 initial submissions, multiple elimination rounds, and extensive cryptanalysis by the global research community [4]:

**ML-KEM (FIPS 203)** — A lattice-based key encapsulation mechanism derived from the CRYSTALS-Kyber submission. ML-KEM is recommended as the primary replacement for RSA and ECDH in key exchange protocols. Its performance is competitive with classical algorithms: key generation and encapsulation are faster than RSA-2048, though ciphertext sizes are approximately 2–3x larger [4].

**ML-DSA (FIPS 204)** — A lattice-based digital signature scheme derived from CRYSTALS-Dilithium. ML-DSA replaces ECDSA and RSA signatures. Signature sizes are larger than classical equivalents (approximately 2.4 KB vs. 64 bytes for ECDSA-256), which has implications for bandwidth-constrained applications [4].

**SLH-DSA (FIPS 205)** — A stateless hash-based signature scheme derived from SPHINCS+. SLH-DSA serves as a conservative backup to lattice-based schemes, relying only on the security of hash functions — a well-understood primitive. Its signatures are larger and slower than ML-DSA but provide a distinct security foundation [4].

A fourth standard, FN-DSA (based on FALCON), is expected in late 2025 for applications requiring compact signatures [4].

## Migration Strategies

### The Hybrid Approach

NIST and major technology vendors recommend a hybrid transition strategy in which classical and post-quantum algorithms run in parallel during the migration period [4][5]. In a hybrid TLS handshake, for example, the session key is derived from both an ECDH exchange and an ML-KEM encapsulation — ensuring security against both classical and quantum adversaries even if one algorithm is later found vulnerable.

Google, Cloudflare, and Apple have already deployed hybrid key exchange (X25519 + ML-KEM-768) in production TLS implementations, demonstrating that the performance overhead is manageable for web-scale traffic [7].

### Migration Priorities

Security experts recommend a phased approach [5]:

1. **Inventory** — Catalog all cryptographic assets: algorithms, key lengths, protocols, and certificate authorities
2. **Prioritize** — Rank systems by data sensitivity and confidentiality horizon. HNDL-vulnerable data (government, healthcare, financial) migrates first
3. **Test** — Validate post-quantum algorithm compatibility with existing infrastructure, paying attention to increased key and signature sizes
4. **Deploy hybrid** — Run classical and PQC algorithms in parallel to maintain backward compatibility
5. **Deprecate classical** — Once confidence in PQC implementations matures, phase out vulnerable classical algorithms

The National Security Agency (NSA) has mandated that all National Security Systems begin transitioning to quantum-resistant algorithms by 2025, with full migration required by 2035 [5].

## Conclusion

The quantum threat to cryptography is not speculative — it is a matter of engineering timeline. The mathematical foundations are established, the standardization process has delivered production-ready algorithms, and nation-state adversaries are presumed to be accumulating encrypted data for future decryption. The organizations that begin migration planning today will face a manageable, phased transition. Those that delay risk a compressed, high-pressure remediation under adversarial conditions. The comparison to Y2K is apt: the problem is solvable, but only if treated with the urgency it demands.
